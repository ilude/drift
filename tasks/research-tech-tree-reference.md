# Tech Tree & Research Mechanics — Game Design Reference

Comparative analysis of research systems across six notable 4X and strategy games, covering structure, resource models, personnel mechanics, and design trade-offs.

---

## 1. Stellaris — Weighted Card Draw

### Structure: Semi-Random Card System

Stellaris replaces a visible tech tree with a **card draw** system. ~400 technologies are divided into three parallel research areas — **Physics, Society, and Engineering** — each with 4 subcategories (12 total). Technologies span 5 tiers plus repeatable endgame techs.

When a research project completes, the player draws a hand of **3 cards** (increasable via perks/civics) from the pool of eligible techs in that area. The player picks one; the unchosen cards go back into the deck. An underlying prerequisite graph exists but is invisible to the player.

### Weighting Mechanics

The deck is heavily stacked. Every tech has a **base draw weight** that varies by tier:

| Tier | Weight Range | Base Cost Range (RP) |
|------|-------------|---------------------|
| 1    | 90–100      | 2,000–3,000         |
| 2    | 70–85       | 4,000–6,000         |
| 3    | 50–65       | 8,000–12,000        |
| 4    | 35–45       | 16,000–24,000       |
| 5    | 20–30       | 32,000–48,000       |
| Repeatable | —    | 50,000 + 5,000/level |

Weight modifiers stack multiplicatively:

- **Repeat draw penalty:** A tech that appeared last hand gets 0.5x weight next draw.
- **Rare tech penalty:** Rare techs get ~0.125x (one-eighth) weight without matching expertise.
- **Scientist expertise match:** Without matching expertise, some techs get 0.1x weight. With it, full weight. This is a 10x swing.
- **Ethics/civics:** Spiritualists get near-zero weight on robotics; Materialists get near-zero on psionic. Militarists boost weapon techs, etc.
- **Federation knowledge sharing:** If a federation ally has the tech, weight doubles.
- **Ascension perks:** Technological Ascendancy gives +50% weight to rare techs.
- **Permanent alternatives:** Debris analysis and events grant "golden border" cards that always appear until researched.

Tier unlocks require 6 techs researched from the previous tier.

### Resource Model

Each area has its own resource (Physics/Society/Engineering research points), generated by:
- Research labs on planets/habitats (primary)
- Research stations over anomalies/resources
- Scientist leader bonuses
- Traditions, edicts, megastructures

Research cost scales with empire size: +0.1% per point of empire size above 100.

### Personnel: Scientists

Scientists are leader characters assigned to each of the three research areas. Key mechanics:

- **Level-based:** Scientists level up over time. Head of Research council position gives +2% research speed per level.
- **Expertise traits:** One per scientist, matching one of the 12 subcategories. Provides +15% research speed for matching techs AND massively influences draw weights (10x swing for some techs). This is the most impactful mechanic — it controls what you *see*, not just how fast you research.
- **Special traits:** Spark of Genius (+flat research speed), Maniacal (boosts psionic/dangerous tech weights), Curator (from Curator enclave).
- **No scientist penalty:** -25% research speed if an area has no assigned scientist.
- **Council stacking:** Multiple scientists on the council stack their research buffs.

### What Makes It Interesting

The card system creates **emergent strategic identity**. Two empires with identical starting conditions will diverge because different cards appear. The scientist expertise system creates a feedback loop — you hire for expertise, which biases your draws, which shapes your empire's capabilities. Swapping scientists mid-game to "fish" for specific techs is a meaningful decision.

### Strengths for Space 4X
- Prevents "solved" optimal build orders — high replayability
- Scientists as characters create narrative attachment
- Expertise system ties personnel to research direction organically
- Permanent alternatives from exploration/combat reward engagement

### Weaknesses
- Opaque — new players don't understand why they're seeing certain techs
- Can feel frustrating when a critical tech never appears
- Invisible prerequisite graph means the player can't plan ahead
- Late-game repeatables become a mindless click

## 2. Civilization VI — Dual Trees with Eureka Boosts

### Structure: Two Parallel Visible Trees

Civ VI splits the traditional tech tree into a **Technology tree** (Science-driven, ~67 techs) and a **Civics tree** (Culture-driven, ~50 civics). Both are traditional prerequisite-based trees with eras as horizontal bands. Some techs are "leaf" nodes with no successors — safe to skip if irrelevant to your situation.

Technologies are organized into 8 eras (Ancient through Information/Future). Era progression is implicit — you research into higher eras as prerequisites are met, not by unlocking era gates.

### The Eureka/Inspiration Boost System

The signature mechanic. Most techs/civics have a **boost condition** — a specific gameplay action that, when completed, instantly provides **50% of the research cost** (40% in Rise & Fall expansion). Examples:

- **Masonry:** Build a Quarry (you were going to do this anyway — or were you?)
- **Sailing:** Found a city on the coast
- **Apprenticeship:** Build 3 mines
- **Rocketry:** Boost a Great Scientist (late-game boosts are harder to trigger naturally)

Key rules:
- Boosts can trigger at any time before the tech completes, even if you're not researching it.
- Each boost triggers only once.
- Some boosts are trivial (build a thing you'd build anyway), others require deliberate effort or exploration.
- Information/Future era techs can only be boosted via Great Scientists or Spies.
- China's unique ability: completing any wonder grants a random Eureka AND Inspiration.
- Babylon's unique ability: Eurekas grant 100% of the remaining cost (!).

Additional boost sources: tribal villages, Great People, espionage (Steal Tech), research alliances, barbarian clan dispersal.

### Tech & Civic Shuffle Mode

An optional game mode that randomizes tech/civic positions *within their era*. You won't find Rocketry in the Medieval Era, but the internal structure of each era is shuffled. Adds replayability to a normally static tree. Future Era is always semi-randomized regardless.

### Resource Model

- **Science** (beakers): Generated by Campus districts, libraries, universities, research labs, Great Scientists, trade routes, city-state bonuses.
- **Culture:** Generated by Theater Square districts, monuments, amphitheaters, etc.
- Both accumulate per-turn and are applied to the current research/civic project.
- No allocation sliders — you research one tech and one civic at a time.

### Personnel

No scientist characters. Great Scientists are one-shot bonuses (boost a tech, grant free tech, etc.) earned through Great People points. The system is impersonal compared to Stellaris.

### What Makes It Interesting

The Eureka system is the standout design. It creates a **feedback loop between gameplay actions and research direction**. Instead of picking a tech and waiting, you're incentivized to:
1. Check what boosts are available for nearby techs
2. Adjust your gameplay to trigger those boosts (build a quarry, settle a coast city, train 3 archers)
3. Research the boosted tech at half cost
4. Use the freed-up science to push further ahead

This rewards flexible, adaptive play over rigid pre-planned tech paths. A player who pivots to capture available boosts will out-research a player who rigidly beelines.

### Strengths for Space 4X
- Eureka system ties research to actual gameplay — brilliant for exploration-heavy games
- Dual trees (tech + culture/society) create interesting resource tension
- Visible tree lets players plan ahead — less frustrating than card draws
- Boost system could translate well to space (survey a gas giant -> boost gas mining tech)

### Weaknesses
- Static tree becomes "solved" after many plays (shuffle mode helps)
- Eureka conditions can feel contrived or checklist-like
- No personnel/scientist mechanic reduces narrative depth
- One-at-a-time research with no allocation creates less interesting resource decisions

## 3. Endless Space 2 — Quad-Tree with Political Flavor

### Structure: Four Visible Quadrants, Five Eras Each

The tech tree is a 2D grid divided into four quadrants:

1. **Military** — Ship weapons, modules, command points, ground troops, manpower
2. **Economy & Trade** — Strategic resource exploitation, system upgrades, marketplace, trade companies
3. **Science & Exploration** — Travel methods, anomaly discovery, terraforming, population capacity
4. **Empire Development** — Ship hulls, diplomacy, food, colonization of extreme planets

Each quadrant has 5 tiers (eras). Advancing to the next era requires researching a **set number of techs from the current era** (not from a specific quadrant — any combination works). This creates meaningful breadth-vs-depth tension.

### Key Mechanics

**Exclusive technologies:** Some tech pairs are mutually exclusive (e.g., energy weapons vs. projectile weapons in Military). You pick one; the other is locked. This can be bypassed by trading with or stealing (hacking) from another faction that chose differently.

**Cost scaling:** Every tech researched increases the cost of all future techs. If 2+ techs finish on the same turn, the cost increase is deferred. Technologies linked by prerequisite lines get a -20% cost discount. Behemoth techs cost +25%.

**Political affinity:** Each tech is tagged with a political party. Researching it increases that party's support in your empire, affecting elections and available laws. This creates a soft feedback loop — researching military techs empowers the Militarist party, which unlocks Militarist laws, which further incentivize military research.

**Science Victory:** Research the four ultimate techs at the far edges of all four quadrants. This forces breadth — you can't win by specializing.

### Faction-Unique Mechanics

Each faction has unique tech variants or replacements:
- **Sophons:** Research techs 50% faster if no other faction has researched them yet. Rewards exploring the tree's edges.
- **Riftborn:** Colonization techs are in reverse order; terraforming is inverted. Start with two Stage 2 techs pre-researched.
- **Cravers:** Unique military techs focused on their consume-and-expand playstyle.

### Resource Model

Science points generated per-system from population, buildings, system improvements, and anomalies. Single science resource applied to one active research project at a time. Heroes assigned to systems or fleets can boost science output.

### What Makes It Interesting

The political affinity system is unique — your research choices shape your government, which shapes your available policies, which incentivize further research. The breadth requirement for era advancement and Science Victory prevents hyper-specialization. Exclusive techs + trading create diplomatic gameplay around tech.

### Strengths for Space 4X
- Four-quadrant structure maps naturally to 4X pillars (eXplore, eXpand, eXploit, eXterminate)
- Exclusive techs create strategic identity without randomness
- Political feedback loop adds depth
- Era gating via breadth requirement prevents one-dimensional play

### Weaknesses
- Visible, static tree becomes solvable
- Cost-per-tech-researched mechanic punishes broad research, contradicting the era requirement
- No scientist characters reduces personnel narrative
- Less replayability than randomized systems

## 4. Distant Worlds 2 — Semi-Hidden Randomized Tree with Location Bonuses

### Structure: Randomized, Partially Hidden Tree

DW2 has three research categories — **Weapons, Energy & Construction, and HighTech & Industrial** — each containing a deep tree of tiered technologies. The key innovation is that the tree is **randomized at game start** and **partially hidden**.

- Only the next available "leaf" technologies are visible. You can't see the full tree.
- Prerequisites are randomized — a tech that required path A in one game might require path B (or both A and B) in another.
- Multiple variants exist at each tier (e.g., 3 different hyperdrive types at tier 3). You may need to research multiple variants to unlock advanced techs.
- "Super" technologies can appear that skip tiers entirely — powerful but require high research bonuses to unlock.

### Research Bonus Thresholds

This is DW2's distinctive mechanic. Advanced techs require a minimum **Research Bonus percentage** in a relevant field before they become researchable. For example, Stealth tech might require 20% Sensor Research bonus.

Research bonuses come from:
- **Research stations** at specific locations (e.g., a nebula might provide Weapon Research bonus)
- **Planetary research facilities**
- **Scientist characters** stationed at research locations
- **Government/racial traits**

This means access to advanced tech depends on your **territorial control and exploration**, not just raw research point output.

### Personnel: Scientists

Scientists are character units that must be physically stationed at research locations:
- Scientist bonuses only apply when at a research station or spaceport with a lab.
- Scientist + station bonuses stack additively (e.g., 8% station + 4% scientist = 12%).
- Multiple scientists at one station: first gives full bonus, second gives half, third gives quarter.
- The game uses the **highest combined bonus** per category across all stations.
- Scientists have personality traits: "Inspiring Presence" boosts everyone at their location; "Demoralizing" does the opposite (fire or exile these).

### Resource Model

Research points come from colonized planets and research modules on stations. Actual research speed depends on:
- Empire strategic value (based on population)
- Game difficulty
- Scientist "Ultra Genius" trait
- Credit investment — projects have an initiation cost in credits and sometimes strategic resources.

### What Makes It Interesting

The Research Bonus threshold system creates a **physical geography of research**. You can't just accumulate beakers — you need to control specific locations, build research stations, and assign the right scientists. The partially hidden tree preserves discovery. The randomized prerequisites prevent solved build orders while the core tech backbone prevents complete crippling.

### Strengths for Space 4X
- Research tied to territorial control and exploration — deeply thematic for space
- Scientist placement as a strategic decision (risk of losing them if station is destroyed)
- Semi-hidden tree preserves joy of discovery
- Research bonus thresholds create natural tech specialization based on geography

### Weaknesses
- Complexity can be overwhelming — the system is hard to parse for new players
- Randomization can feel unfair if critical paths are blocked
- The automation layer (DW2 has optional AI advisors) can make research decisions for you, undermining engagement
- Multiple interacting systems (bonuses, thresholds, costs, prerequisites) create opacity

## 5. Master of Orion 1 & 2 — Forced Exclusion Creates Identity

### MOO1: Randomized Trees with Forced Choices

#### Structure

Six technology categories: **Computers, Construction, Force Fields, Planetology, Propulsion, and Weapons.** Each has tech levels 1–50, grouped into 10 sub-tiers of 5 levels each. Within each sub-tier, there may be 1–4 available technologies.

The tree is **randomized at game start**: roughly half of non-core techs are removed entirely. If a sub-tier has only one tech, it's mandatory. If multiple are available, you pick one to research (or research multiple before advancing — your choice, unlike MOO2).

#### Resource Model

Colony production is split via sliders across: ships, defense, industry, ecology, and **research**. Research points from all colonies pool into a single total, then a second set of sliders allocates that pool across the six tech categories.

Research doesn't guarantee completion — once base cost is met, you get an increasing **percentage chance** per turn of completion, rising with continued investment. This creates a soft, probabilistic completion curve rather than a hard point threshold.

#### Miniaturization

A brilliant side mechanic: technologies shrink and get cheaper as your overall tech level in that category increases. A weapon that fills half a ship at tech level 10 might fit in a quarter at tech level 20. This rewards broad advancement even when no new techs are immediately useful.

#### Racial Modifiers

Races have category affinities: being "excellent" in a field means 60% of normal research cost and more techs available; being "poor" means 125% cost and fewer options. This creates asymmetric starting positions.

### MOO2: One Pick Per Tier, Full Visibility

MOO2 changed the formula: all techs are visible, and you pick exactly **one tech per tier** in each category. Unchosen techs are locked unless obtained via trade, theft, or conquest.

The **Creative** racial trait bypasses this entirely (you get all techs at each tier), making it one of the most powerful traits in the game. **Uncreative** removes your choice — you get a random one.

### What Makes MOO1/2 Interesting

MOO1's randomized removal means you genuinely don't know what's available. You might lack shields entirely, forcing you to lean into armor and point defense. You might get amazing propulsion but terrible weapons, making you a fast-moving diplomatic power. The game is about **adapting to what you got**, not executing a pre-planned path.

MOO2's one-pick system is more controlled but creates permanent strategic identity: you chose beam weapons over missiles, so your ship designs, fleet doctrines, and diplomacy (trading for what you lack) all follow from that choice.

### Strengths for Space 4X
- MOO1's randomization creates genuinely unique playthroughs
- Forced exclusion makes tech choices *matter* — you can't have everything
- Miniaturization rewards long-term tech investment beyond just "unlock new thing"
- Trade/theft as the only way to get excluded techs creates diplomatic gameplay
- Slider-based allocation creates interesting resource management

### Weaknesses
- MOO1's randomization can be too punitive (no shields = rough)
- MOO2's one-pick system can feel rote once optimal picks are known per tier
- MOO2's Creative trait is so strong it warps the meta
- Probabilistic completion (MOO1) is unintuitive — "why isn't this done yet?"

## 6. Sword of the Stars — Per-Race Probability Trees

### Structure: Cascading Random Rolls at Game Start

SotS has the most aggressive randomization of any game in this list. At game initialization, the game rolls through every branch of the tech tree sequentially. For each link between techs, it rolls against a **race-specific probability**. On failure, that link and everything beyond it is severed.

If a later branch links back into a severed branch, it can "rescue" previously severed techs. The result is that each player gets a unique subset of the full tree, shaped by their race's probability weights.

### Per-Race Probability Weights

Each tech has per-race percentages. Example (armor progression cumulative probabilities):

| Tech | Human | Hiver | Tarka | Liir | Zuul | Morrigi |
|------|-------|-------|-------|------|------|---------|
| Polysilicate Alloys | 95% | 99% | 100% | 99% | 78% | 99% |
| Magnoceramic | 82% | 92% | 95% | 75% | 36% | 79% |
| Quark Resonators | 49% | 64% | 76% | 38% | 11% | 31% |
| Adamantite Alloys | 20% | 32% | 30% | 11% | 2% | 6% |

Core technologies (~30% of the tree) are guaranteed for all races — these are the minimum viable techs to play the game. The remaining ~70% is subject to dice rolls. In practice, you get roughly 70% of the total tree, but *which* 70% varies every game.

### Hidden Information

You don't see the full tree — only the next available techs. You don't know if a tech leads anywhere until you research it and its successors appear (or don't). There's no indication which techs are random vs. core. Each game is opaque.

### Salvaging as Safety Net

The only way to get techs not in your tree: **salvage** them from enemy ship wreckage after battle, or receive them via allied tech trades. This creates a strong incentive for aggressive play — even losing a battle can yield valuable salvage if you recover the wreckage.

### Resource Model

Research points generated by population and research-focused planetary improvements. Single research queue — you pick one tech at a time. Cost scales with tech tier. No allocation sliders between categories.

### What Makes It Interesting

The cascading probability system means that even two players of the same race will have different trees. A Hiver player with 99% chance of getting Polysilicate Alloys still has a 1% chance of not getting it — and when that happens, their entire strategy shifts. The hidden tree preserves genuine discovery. The salvage mechanic turns combat into a research activity.

The per-race weights create **racial identity through capability** rather than just stats. Zuul are bad at research not because they have fewer research points, but because their probability weights are lower — they're less likely to *have* advanced techs. The Tarka are reliable generalists with high weights across the board. The Liir excel in specific areas but have gaps.

### Strengths for Space 4X
- Maximum replayability — no two games are the same, even with the same race
- Salvage mechanic brilliantly ties combat to research
- Per-race weights create organic racial identity
- Hidden tree preserves sense of discovery
- Forces adaptation rather than optimization

### Weaknesses
- Can be extremely punishing — no shields AND no point defense = death
- Players have no agency over what's available, only what to research from what's offered
- Hard to plan long-term when the tree is hidden
- Frustrating for players who want to execute specific strategies
- The "joy" of discovery can become the "frustration" of absence

## Comparative Summary

| Aspect | Stellaris | Civ VI | Endless Space 2 | Distant Worlds 2 | MOO 1/2 | SotS |
|--------|-----------|--------|-----------------|------------------|---------|------|
| **Structure** | Hidden card draw | Dual visible trees | 4-quadrant visible grid | Semi-hidden random | Random removal + forced picks | Cascading probability rolls |
| **Randomness** | Per-draw weighted | Static (shuffle mode optional) | Static | Per-game prerequisites | Per-game removal | Per-game per-race probability |
| **Visibility** | Next 3 options only | Full tree | Full tree | Next available leaves | Available techs in current tier | Next available only |
| **Exclusivity** | None (all eventually available) | None | Some exclusive pairs | Variant selection | One per tier (MOO2) / random removal (MOO1) | Probability-based removal |
| **Personnel** | Scientists with expertise + traits | Great Scientists (one-shot) | Heroes (generic) | Scientists at stations | None | None |
| **Resource Split** | 3 parallel resources | 2 parallel (Science + Culture) | Single science pool | Single + credit costs + bonus thresholds | Slider allocation across 6 categories | Single queue |
| **Exploration Link** | Debris analysis -> permanent cards | Eurekas from gameplay actions | Curiosities reveal new options | Location bonuses gate advanced tech | Trade/theft from rivals | Salvage from combat |
| **Replayability** | High (weighted draws vary) | Medium (static tree, boosts add variety) | Medium (static, faction variety helps) | High (randomized prerequisites) | High (MOO1) / Medium (MOO2) | Very High (per-race probability) |

## Design Observations for a Space Exploration Sim

### Patterns Worth Considering

1. **Exploration-research coupling** is the strongest thematic fit for a space 4X. Civ VI's Eurekas (survey a gas giant -> boost atmospheric processing), DW2's location bonuses (research station at a nebula enables better sensors), and SotS's salvage mechanic all tie the "explore" pillar to the "research" pillar. The best designs make research feel like part of exploration, not a separate screen.

2. **Forced exclusion creates identity.** MOO1/2, SotS, and ES2's exclusive pairs all demonstrate that meaningful research choices require giving something up. When you can eventually get everything (Stellaris, Civ VI), the choices are about *order*, not *identity*. When some things are permanently unavailable, your empire becomes distinct.

3. **Personnel matter.** Stellaris's scientist expertise system is the gold standard — scientists don't just speed things up, they shape what's available. DW2's physical placement of scientists at stations is brilliant for a space sim. Personnel create narrative, attachment, and emergent storytelling.

4. **Visibility is a design knob.** Full visibility (Civ VI, ES2) enables planning but reduces discovery. Hidden trees (SotS, DW2) create discovery but frustrate planners. Stellaris's card system is a middle ground — you can't see the full tree, but you see your immediate options. The right choice depends on whether the game rewards planning or adaptation.

5. **Multiple resource types create tension.** Stellaris's 3-way split (Physics/Society/Engineering) and Civ VI's Science/Culture split both create genuine "which do I invest in?" decisions. A single science pool (ES2, DW2) reduces this tension. MOO1's 6-way slider allocation is the richest but most overwhelming.

6. **Cost scaling matters.** ES2's "every tech researched increases all future costs" creates a breadth penalty. Stellaris's empire-size scaling creates a tall-vs-wide tension. MOO1's miniaturization creates a reason to research broadly even without new unlocks. The cost model shapes behavior as much as the tree structure.
